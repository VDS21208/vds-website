This guide is written for Canadian security and operations buyers evaluating mobile surveillance — whether the eventual vendor is Vision Detection Systems, a Canadian operator like Zedcor, or someone else entirely. We deploy across 30+ US states and are planning a Canadian launch. The framing below reflects what we've learned operating at scale and what Canadian operators have taught us about their market.

## How mobile surveillance differs in Canada

Canadian mobile surveillance has a few structural differences from the US market:

- **Cross-border data is a contract-level question.** Many Canadian buyers — especially Crown corporations, regulated utilities, and Quebec-based organizations — require Canadian data residency. A US vendor running US-region cloud infrastructure will not pass these reviews without a Canadian data plane.
- **Provincial privacy law adds a layer.** PIPEDA is the federal default, but Alberta, British Columbia, and Quebec each operate substantially similar provincial regimes that take precedence in their jurisdictions. Quebec Law 25 is the strictest and now includes meaningful penalties.
- **Bilingual support is jurisdiction-specific.** Quebec deployments typically require French-language operator and SOC capability. Most provinces don't, but the major Canadian operators offer bilingual coverage as table stakes.
- **Hardware logistics span vast distances.** A unit deployed in Northern BC or Northern Ontario may be a 10-hour drive from the nearest service tech. Service-level agreements need to reflect that geography honestly.

## PIPEDA and provincial privacy frameworks

A mobile surveillance program in Canada has to handle personal information lawfully under PIPEDA at minimum. The relevant questions for a buyer:

- **Notice.** Are you posting visible notice at the deployment site that video monitoring is in effect, with contact information for inquiries?
- **Purpose limitation.** Is footage retained only as long as the stated business purpose requires? 30–90 days is the typical defensible range absent a specific incident.
- **Cross-border disclosure.** If footage leaves Canada — even to a US-based SOC for review — your privacy notice must say so, and your contracts must address it.
- **Quebec specifically.** Law 25 requires a designated privacy officer, mandatory breach notification, and Privacy Impact Assessments for projects involving personal information. A Quebec deployment is not a copy-paste of an Ontario deployment.

Alberta PIPA and BC PIPA each have their own breach notification triggers and operator-of-record rules. Treat them as separate compliance reviews, not as PIPEDA clones.

## Cold-weather and solar performance in Canada

Solar-autonomous mobile surveillance works in Canada, including in winter. Three caveats:

- **Operating temperature.** Mobile Surveillance Unit is rated to -20F (-29C). That covers the vast majority of Canadian populated regions year-round. Extreme cold beyond that range is a hardware spec question, not a "does it work" question.
- **Solar irradiance in winter.** Short December days at northern latitudes mean battery sizing matters more than panel wattage. A well-sized system rides through 5–7 day overcast stretches at Vancouver or Toronto latitudes. Further north, sizing changes.
- **Snow load and panel orientation.** Steeper panel angles shed snow better. This is a deployment-time configuration, not a hardware redesign.

Cold weather is not the reason VDS isn't in Canada yet. Operations and compliance are.

## NDAA equivalence and Canadian government procurement

Section 889 of the US NDAA prohibits certain Chinese-manufactured surveillance equipment in federal contracts. It is a US statute and does not apply in Canada.

Canada's federal procurement uses different supply-chain integrity tools, including the Policy on Government Security, the Integrated Cyber Security Risk Management Framework, and case-by-case national security reviews. There is no single "Canadian NDAA." Crown procurement increasingly asks vendors to attest to supply chain provenance, and major Canadian operators have responded accordingly.

If you are buying for a Canadian federal department or Crown corporation, the procurement instrument — not a generic compliance certificate — defines the bar.

## What to ask any mobile surveillance vendor in Canada

Use these questions on every vendor call, ours included:

1. Where is video stored, and in which cloud region?
2. Where is the SOC physically located, and what languages do operators speak?
3. What is the retention policy, and can it be configured per site?
4. How do you handle a PIPEDA data-subject access request?
5. What is your mean response time for a verified intrusion alert, and how is that measured?
6. What is the solar autonomy window for the configuration you're quoting at this site's latitude?
7. Who owns the hardware at end of term, and what is the removal process?
8. Can you provide three Canadian references in the same vertical at similar scale?
